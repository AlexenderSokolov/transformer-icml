## 4. A finite phase set with uniform length and component count

Put rho=1/1000 and alpha=lambda-floor(lambda). On 0<=r<=1/3 define

    q_alpha(r)={3r-2alpha}, H_alpha(r)=q_alpha(r)-2r,

where {z}=z-floor(z). The variable q_alpha here is a phase, not the query token. Since 3r covers exactly one period of the fractional-part function,

    integral_0^(1/3) q_alpha(r) dr=1/6,
    integral_0^(1/3) H_alpha(r) dr=1/6-1/9=1/18.             (4.1)

For precision, the first identity follows by substituting y=3r-2alpha: the integral of {y} over any interval of length one is 1/2, by splitting at integers and integrating y-floor(y) on each piece. Thus (4.1) holds for every alpha, including zero and phases with endpoint jumps.

Let E0={r in [0,1/3]: H_alpha(r)>=1/12}. Since H_alpha<=1, its length m obeys

    1/18<=m+(1/3-m)/12=1/36+11m/12,
    m>=1/33.                                              (4.2)

Remove points where the distance of r or q_alpha(r) to an integer is less than rho. The r removal has length at most rho because r lies in [0,1/3]. The q removal has length exactly 2rho/3 before intersecting E0: q traverses one full period at slope 3. Therefore the retained set E satisfies

    |E|>=1/33-5rho/3=63/2200>1/40.                         (4.3)

There is a uniform finite component bound. In the interior of [0,1/3], the function q_alpha has at most one jump, since its argument runs through a length-one interval. On each of the at most two resulting pieces, q_alpha is affine with slope 3 and H_alpha is affine with slope 1. The constraints H_alpha>=1/12, r>=rho, and rho<=q_alpha<=1-rho each cut out an interval in that piece. The other r-distance constraint is automatic. Thus E is a union of at most two intervals, with endpoints open or closed as dictated by the formulas; isolated points may be discarded without altering (4.3). No infinite set of hidden components is used.

Define for a real w

    r={lambda-w}, q_alpha(r)={lambda-3w}.                   (4.4)

To verify the second equality, write lambda=m+alpha and lambda-w=k+r. Then lambda-3w=3k-2m+3r-2alpha. The integer part drops out. On any half-open unit interval [k,k+1) in w, the map w->{lambda-w} traverses a complete period and is a length-preserving reflection on at most two pieces. The set of w for which r belongs to E therefore has length |E|>1/40 and at most four interval components in that unit interval. This remains true for arbitrary noninteger lambda and for integer lambda. No distributional assertion about phases is involved.

## 10. RATE and the precision iff, including oscillating sequences

Fix K>=2. Let (h_M,g_M) be any public deterministic source-independent sequence legal for every integer M>=1. All statements here refer to the same F and its same-trajectory uniform actual risk. First suppose lambda_M->infinity. Then T_M->0 and phi(lambda_M)->0. Eventually lambda_M>=2^48 and T_M+phi(lambda_M)<1. ENVELOPE therefore gives

    R_K(M;h_M,g_M)
      =Theta_K((M+1)^(-1/2)+ln(lambda_M)/lambda_M).         (10.1 RATE)

To spell out the comparison, T_M=K^(5/2)(M+1)^(-1/2), so the lower bound is at least 2^-42 times the displayed sum, and the upper is at most max(64K^(5/2),1024K^5) times it. The onset can depend on the sequence; these comparison constants depend only on K. This is an order statement for E[TV], not an almost-sure, fixed-row, or conditional risk statement.

We now remove the initial lambda_M->infinity assumption and prove the complete equivalence

    limsup_M sqrt(M+1)R_K(M;h_M,g_M)<infinity
     iff
    liminf_M G_M/[M sqrt(M+1)ln(M+1)]>0.                 (10.2 OPT-PREC)

For necessity write m=M+1. If the left side is finite, there is a finite positive A such that R_K<=A/sqrt(m) for all sufficiently large M. Thus R_K->0. The general-configuration theorem 024 applies to this exact legal public sequence and forces lambda_M->infinity. This is the only use of 024; its one particular coarse example would not suffice.

ENVELOPE then has untruncated lower term eventually, as in (10.1). Increasing a constant if necessary, choose C>=1 with

    sqrt(m) ln(lambda_M)/lambda_M<=C                     (10.3)

for every sufficiently large M; C=max(1,2^42 A) works. The function phi(x) is decreasing for x>e because phi'(x)=(1-ln x)/x^2. Since lambda_M->infinity, eventually lambda_M>e. At any such index with lambda_M<=sqrt(m), monotonicity would give

    sqrt(m)phi(lambda_M)>=sqrt(m)phi(sqrt(m))=(1/2)ln m.

For all m with (1/2)ln m>C this contradicts (10.3). Therefore lambda_M>sqrt(m) for every sufficiently large M, even if the sequence oscillates. In particular ln(lambda_M)>=(1/2)ln m, and (10.3) yields

    lambda_M>=sqrt(m)ln m/(2C),
    liminf_M lambda_M/[sqrt(m)ln m]>=1/(2C)>0.            (10.4)

This is exactly the right side of (10.2), since lambda_M=G_M/M. It holds eventually at all indices, not only along a selected subsequence.

Conversely, the right side of (10.2), including a possible infinite liminf, provides c>0 such that lambda_M>=c sqrt(m)ln m for every sufficiently large M. Hence lambda_M->infinity. Put ell_m=c sqrt(m)ln m; for large m it exceeds e. Monotonicity gives

    sqrt(m)phi(lambda_M)
      <=sqrt(m)phi(ell_m)
      =[ln c+(1/2)ln m+ln ln m]/(c ln m).                (10.5)

The right side is bounded by 2/c for all sufficiently large m. Explicitly one can take ln m>=max(2,2|ln c|) and ell_m>e; then |ln c|/ln m<=1/2 and ln ln m/ln m<=1. ENVELOPE gives

    sqrt(m)R_K<=64K^(5/2)+(2048/c)K^5

at all sufficiently large indices. This proves sufficiency without an upper bound or monotonicity assumption on lambda_M. The constants may depend on K and the public sequence, never on the hidden source or index M.

Since M^(3/2)<=M sqrt(M+1)<=sqrt(2)M^(3/2) for M>=1, the positive eventual bound in (10.4) is equivalent to

    G_M=Omega(M^(3/2)ln(M+1)).                            (10.6)

## 11. A full-M legal integer rule attaining the order

Use h=h0 and define, for every M>=1,
