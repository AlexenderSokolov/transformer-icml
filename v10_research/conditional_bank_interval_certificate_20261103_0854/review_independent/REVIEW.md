## Certificate and batch context

Then the float optimum is at most max_b(H_b+d_b), while any legal witness w is at least L_bank(w)−d_bank(w). Subtracting proves (A); substituting a finite actual witness score y from the **same prescribed batch and slot** proves (B). Taking max with zero preserves the upper bound. These are mathematical conditional inequalities, not evidence that NumPy or its transcendental functions meet any particular d_b.

A strict winner-bank gap excludes all other banks, but not a different legal word inside the winning bank. A unique maximum additionally needs proof that the bank contains exactly one legal word or a valid upper bound on every same-bank alternative. A minimum-cost bank representative alone cannot establish that uniqueness; the scope addendum makes this explicit. ## Rational interval proof and example

For reduced 0≤y≤1/2, the exponential Taylor series has nonnegative terms and its tail after N is bounded by the first omitted term divided by 1−y/(N+2). Repeated positive squaring preserves endpoint order; negative arguments use reciprocals in reverse order. The monotone exponential formulas for tanh and sigmoid then give valid rational enclosures. For log, scaling to m∈[1,2] gives y=(m−1)/(m+1)∈[0,1/3]; the odd-power atanh expansion and the displayed geometric upper tail are valid, and reciprocation reverses/negates endpoints for x<1. The CE interval's signs correctly reverse the log bounds. For t=0 or 1, a coefficient can vanish without any 0·infinity ambiguity because the relevant q intervals are strictly inside (0,1); in the actual demo t=1/4 and e=1/16. Coarse enclosures must be refined if log arguments reach an endpoint.

Its three enclosures, each narrower than 1e-25, overlap reconstructed author enclosures; the author v2 script's stdout also replays byte-identically. The only legal words at M=2,k=1 are 0000,1000,0100, with respective banks (2,0,1,0),(1,0,0,0),(0,0,0,0), so the first bank really is a singleton. The v[0]=2^-20 change gives an ideal gap at most 2^-21<2/1000; independent admissible signed score errors could reverse the pair, but this is not evidence that NumPy actually does so.
