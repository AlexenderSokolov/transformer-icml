Independent source review: objectives and ergodicity

Source: Kağan Akman, Jake Feldman Starosta, Naci Saldi, and Serdar Yüksel, When can Optimally Trained Transformers Achieve Near-Lossless Token Prediction for non-Markovian Data?, arXiv:2610.41038v1, full paper. Review completed 2026-11-03 UTC.

Scope and limitation. This is a focused source audit, not a complete verification of the paper or a proof that Theorem 26 or Theorem 27 is false. The counterexample below concerns an implication used by the proof under a literal reading of a displayed assumption; it is not presented as a counterexample to the complete theorem. The training-objective example is an abstract coupled-output model, not the paper’s full Transformer architecture.

1. Frequency-weighted empirical loss versus the dynamic program

What the paper says

  • Section 3.2 starts from K̃ samples, which may contain duplicate prompts, then constructs a dataset D indexed by K unique feature vectors. The label yᵏ is the empirical conditional distribution of next tokens among the nₖ original occurrences of prompt k. It is a normalized probability measure; its definition does not retain the occurrence count as a multiplicative weight.
  • Section 3.3, Equation (15), defines VCE = Σₖ (nₖ/K̃) CE(yᵏ, νᵏ) and explicitly identifies it with ordinary empirical cross-entropy over all K̃ original samples.
  • Section 3.5 says the finite model uses the Equation (15) cross-entropy, but Equation (16) displays the terminal cost (1/K) Σₖ ĈE(μ̂Tᵏ,ᴺ, ŷᵏ). There is one term per unique prompt, not one per occurrence. The paper does not provide a clear definition there of the hatted CE that explicitly restores nₖ/K̃; its displayed arguments have no explicit count.
  • Appendix A, Algorithm 1, lines 5–9, computes ŷᵏ = Rl(yᵏ) and forms D from K unique prompts with quantized labels. Line 11 writes (1/K) Σₖ CE(yᵏ, softmax(EUxT,γᵏ,ᴸ)), using an un-hatted y even though line 9 used ŷ. Thus the algorithm also has an apparent label-notation discrepancy, separate from the weights.
  • Section 5 states that the policy returned by Equation (16) minimizes VCE, and identifies it as a minimizer of the frequency-weighted excess KL loss E. Appendix D.2, Steps 4–5, uses the optimality inequality E(γ*) ≤ E(Û), Equation (42). Appendix D.3, Step 3, relies on those steps to prove Theorem 26. The stated argument does not establish this inequality from the differently displayed terminal cost.

Source locations: full paper, §§3.2, 3.3, 3.5 and 5, Appendix A Algorithm 1, and Appendix D.2–D.3.

Inference and reproducible illustration

On the definitions as written, nₖ/K̃ and 1/K are different unless counts are equal. A normalized empirical label does not itself supply the missing multiplicity. For an elementary coupled-output example, take two distinct prompts with occurrence weights 0.9 and 0.1 and empirical token-1 probabilities 0.9 and 0.1, respectively. Restrict the model to output the same token-1 probability q for both prompts. Cross-entropy is uniquely minimized at the target mixture probability: the frequency-weighted objective gives q = 0.9·0.9 + 0.1·0.1 = 0.82, while the uniform prompt objective gives q = (0.9 + 0.1)/2 = 0.5. All values are exact; no random experiment is used.

This establishes that equivalence of minimizers does not follow algebraically when predictions are coupled. It does not show that these two costs have different minimizers for the full architecture under the theorem’s hypotheses. Exact simultaneous interpolation or equal counts can make minimizers coincide. A corrected weighted terminal cost or a separate proof for uniform-prompt optimization may resolve the discrepancy; such a repair would also need to account for any target quantization.

Quantization qualification. Equation (16) and Algorithm 1 also refer to quantized labels, whereas Equation (15) and the excess-KL proof use yᵏ. Theorem 26’s displayed refinement limit lists n, m and β, not l. Definition 32 requires M to divide l − 1; that condition alone does not force l → ∞ for fixed M as n, m and β grow. This is a point needing clarification or a separate bound, not a demonstrated violation of the theorem. Source: full paper, §5.1, Appendix A and Appendix C, Definition 32.

2. One-site averages and shift ergodicity

What the paper says

  • In §2.1, Assumption 6 calls the finite-state latent process ergodic “i.e.” its time average of f(Zᵣ), for every f on S, converges almost surely to the expectation under πZ; it additionally assumes stationarity.
  • In Appendix B.1, Lemma 30, the paper asserts stationarity and ergodicity of the observation process and argues that a measurable function of the latent process and independent iid noise is ergodic.
  • In Appendix D.3, Step 2, the proof of Theorem 26 invokes this assertion and Birkhoff’s theorem for indicators of length-(M+1) quantized blocks. It concludes nw/K̃ → p(w) and joint block frequencies → p(w) P̂(v|w), almost surely.
  • Section 2.2 introduces Markov assumptions for a separate hidden-Markov case; Theorem 26 expressly assumes Assumptions 3 and 6, not §2.2’s Markov hypotheses. Assumption 3 requires a strictly positive and Lipschitz emission density and positive reference measure for every quantization cell.

Source locations: full paper, §2 Assumption 3, §2.1 Assumption 6, §2.2, §5.1 Theorem 26, Appendix B.1 Lemma 30, and Appendix D.3 Step 2.

Analytic example satisfying the displayed one-site condition

Let S = Y = {0,1}, with the discrete metric and reference measure μ({0}) = μ({1}) = 1/2. Select a component C ∈ {0,1} with probability 1/2 each. Conditional on C=0, let Z be iid fair Bernoulli. Conditional on C=1, choose a fair initial phase and let Z alternate deterministically forever. Both component laws are stationary, so their mixture is stationary. In either component, each symbol has limiting frequency 1/2 almost surely: by the law of large numbers for the iid component and by direct counting for the alternating component. Consequently, for every f:S→ℝ,

(1/T) Σr=0T−1 f(Zᵣ) → [f(0)+f(1)]/2 almost surely.

Thus the displayed one-site average holds with πZ uniform, although the mixture is not shift-ergodic: its components have different long-run pair statistics. The source is not required to be a finite-order Markov chain in the general stationary branch.

Generate each Yᵣ from Zᵣ through independent binary symmetric noise with flip probability 1/4, independently across times and from the entire latent process. The density ψ(y|z) relative to μ is 3/2 if y=z and 1/2 otherwise. Hence Assumption 3(A1) holds with qmin = 1/2; (A2) holds with Lψ = 1 for the discrete metric. For every s choose the valid 1/s-net {0,1}, whose quantizer is the identity; each cell has μ-mass 1/2, so (A3) holds with μmins = 1/2. The observation channel is memoryless with independent emissions as required in the paper’s setup.

For the observable adjacent-equality event, under C=0, Y is iid fair and P(Yᵣ=Yᵣ₊₁|C=0)=1/2. Under C=1, latent neighbors differ, so observed neighbors agree exactly when one and only one channel flip occurs; its probability is 2·(1/4)·(3/4)=3/8. The unconditional ensemble probability is (1/2)(1/2)+(1/2)(3/8)=7/16. The empirical adjacent-equality frequency converges almost surely to 1/2 in the first component and 3/8 in the second, not to 7/16. This follows from the strong law for iid observations in the first component and, in the second, the strong law applied separately to even and odd pair positions (each subsequence uses disjoint iid noises). No simulation or random-number generator is involved.

This directly refutes the inference from the displayed one-site condition plus Assumption 3 to the ensemble window-frequency convergence asserted in Appendix D.3. It also shows positive emission probabilities alone do not force ergodicity. If “ergodic” is instead intended in the standard shift-invariant-process sense, then the displayed formula is only a consequence rather than a complete definition, and the word and section heading plausibly supply the stronger assumption. Under standard shift ergodicity, independent iid noise and measurable factors give the window ergodicity that the proof invokes. The issue should therefore be presented as an assumption/definition ambiguity and, on the literal displayed-condition reading, a proof gap—not as proof that the full theorem is false.
